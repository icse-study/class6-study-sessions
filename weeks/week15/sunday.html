🔬 Science & Space

🐧 A new penguin species — 21 Sep

Scientists named the Southeastern gentoo penguin (Pygoscelis kerguelensis) — the first new penguin species in more than 100 years. It lives on the remote Kerguelen Islands in the Southern Ocean. By reading the whole genome (DNA) of 64 penguins, researchers found that what looked like one gentoo species is really four.

💡 A look-alike species that only DNA can tell apart is called a cryptic species.

🌗 Autumnal equinox — 23 Sep

On the equinox the Sun is directly over the Equator, so day and night are nearly equal (about 12 hours each) all over the Earth. After 23 September, days in the Northern Hemisphere (including India) get shorter than nights until the March equinox.

💡 Equinox comes from Latin: aequus (equal) + nox (night).

🚀 Starship stacked for orbit — 23 Sep

SpaceX stacked its giant Starship ship on the Super Heavy booster at Starbase, Texas, making a rocket about 124 metres tall. Flight 14, planned for 28 September, is its first try at reaching a full orbit around the Earth.

🦏 World Rhino Day — 22 Sep

A day to protect all five rhino species. India is home to most of the world's greater one-horned rhinos, and Kaziranga National Park in Assam has the largest population.

🎨 Arts & Culture

📚 Booker Prize shortlist — 22 Sep

Six novels made the shortlist for the Booker Prize 2026, one of the world's top prizes for a novel written in English. The judges are led by historian Mary Beard. The winner, announced on 9 November, gets £50,000.

💡 Booker = novels · Emmys = TV · Oscars = films · Grammys = music.

🌊 Nadi Utsav celebrates the Yamuna — 24–26 Sep

The Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts (IGNCA) held its 7th Nadi Utsav (river festival) in Delhi. This year it was dedicated to the Yamuna — its history, culture and ecology.

🕊️ Peace Day & Tourism Day — 21 & 27 Sep

The United Nations marks the International Day of Peace on 21 September and World Tourism Day on 27 September.

🏅 Sports

🏏 Cricket gold for India's women — 22 Sep

India's first gold of the Asian Games came in women's cricket. India made 216/3 (Smriti Mandhana 79 off 45 balls) and bowled out Sri Lanka for 69 to win by 147 runs. Deepti Sharma took 3 wickets for 6 runs. India also won this gold in 2023.

🏋️ Mirabai Chanu's silver — 23 Sep

Mirabai Chanu won silver in the women's 49 kg weightlifting with a total lift of 198 kg — her first Asian Games medal. It was India's first weightlifting medal at the Asian Games in 28 years, since Karnam Malleswari in 1998.

🎯 First shooting gold — 25 Sep

Suruchi Singh and Kamaljeet won the 10 m air pistol mixed team gold with an Asian record of 484.6, beating South Korea. It was India's first-ever Asian Games gold in this event.

🤼 Kabaddi double gold

Both Indian kabaddi teams beat Iran in the finals: the men won 40–34 for India's ninth Asian Games kabaddi title, and the women won 37–34. Sawan Barwal added a silver in the men's marathon. By 26 September India had 30 medals: 4 gold, 12 silver and 14 bronze.

♟️ Chess Olympiad — final day 27 Sep

At the 46th Chess Olympiad in Samarkand, Uzbekistan, India's Open team went into the last round second, just one match point behind host Uzbekistan, after beating Uzbekistan's second team 3.5–0.5 with wins by Gukesh, Praggnanandhaa and Nihal Sarin. India's final-round opponent is Hungary.

Minerals — Rocks, Minerals & Ores — think and write
Wednesday's topic, one level deeper. Write each answer in your notebook first (4–6 lines), then open the model answer and compare. Then take the 10-question Geo Quiz.
1. What are rocks? How are rocks different from minerals?
👀 Show model answer
Rocks are the solid materials that make up the Earth's crust. Minerals are naturally occurring inorganic substances with definite properties and composition. A rock is made of minerals — one (limestone) or several (granite). So minerals are the building blocks; rocks are the combinations.
2. What are ores? Give two examples.
👀 Show model answer
Rocks containing metallic minerals from which metals are extracted. Examples: iron ore, manganese ore (also bauxite for aluminium).
3. Trace the use of minerals from the Stone Age to modern times.
👀 Show model answer
Stone Age — flint for tools and fire. Bronze Age — alloy of copper and tin, start of the age of metals. Iron Age followed. Mining grew from small-scale hand mining to large-scale machine mining; today minerals power industries, transport and construction.
4. Why are minerals called non-renewable? What must we do about it?
👀 Show model answer
They take millions of years to form, so once used they cannot be replaced in our lifetime. We must use them judiciously, recycle metals, reduce waste and use alternatives.
5. Who is a geologist? Why would one be consulted while building a bridge?
👀 Show model answer
A scientist who studies the Earth's structure, substance, history and processes. Before building a bridge, a geologist checks whether the rock below is strong and stable enough to carry the load.
6. Name five common rock-forming minerals and four physical properties of minerals.
👀 Show model answer
Minerals: iron, silica, quartz, feldspar, mica (also nickel, magnesium, sulphur, calcium). Properties: colour, hardness, lustre, texture.
7. How has large-scale mining affected mineral reserves?
👀 Show model answer
Machines extract minerals far faster than hand mining, so reserves have diminished considerably; since minerals are non-renewable, they may run out.
8. Explain how marble is formed and why it is valued.
👀 Show model answer
Limestone is changed by heat and pressure into marble, a more compact rock. It is strong, smooth and beautiful, so craftspeople have used it for statues, floors and monuments — like the Taj Mahal.
9. Observe and describe ways rocks are used in your neighbourhood. Could they be misused?
👀 Show model answer
Granite for kitchen tops and steps, gravel and sand for concrete, stone for walls and roads. Misuse: illegal sand mining from rivers, wasteful quarrying, dumping rock powder that pollutes air and water.
10. "Minerals are the building blocks of the Earth." Explain.
👀 Show model answer
Every rock in the crust is made of minerals. Mountains, plateaus, soils and even sand are made of minerals such as quartz, feldspar and mica, so the Earth's solid surface is literally built from them.
