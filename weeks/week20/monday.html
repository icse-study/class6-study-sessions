🔁 Quick recap

Saviours of the Train (E. Nesbit) — Bobbie, Peter and Phyllis see a landslide block the line and stop the 11:29 with red scarves on sticks.
Gathering Leaves (Robert Frost) — leaves weigh next to nothing and are next to useless, yet “a crop is a crop”.
The Butterfly Mountain’s Shadow (Tara Gandhi) — a night climb of Adam’s Peak, butterflies on pilgrimage and the peak’s triangular shadow at dawn.
Across the Border (Sophie Jewett) — a day in fairyland of golden flowers and white birds; home at night to brown birds and candlelight.

🧩 Links between the texts

Nature: a landslide, falling leaves, migrating butterflies, an imaginary green fairyland.
Magic in the real: Phyllis thinks the railway is “enchanted”; the mountain’s shadow “appeared like magic”.
Effort and reward: the children’s race, the leaf gatherer’s endless loading, the narrator’s exhausting climb.
Journeys home: Bobbie longs to go home; the poet creeps home at nightfall.

📚 Author quick-check

Edith Nesbit — The Railway Children, Five Children and It, The Story of the Treasure Seekers, The Wouldbegoods · Robert Frost — “The Road Not Taken”, “Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening”, “Fire and Ice”, “Mending Wall”, “Birches” · Tara Gandhi — author and wildlife conservationist · Sophie Jewett — The Pilgrim, and Other Poems, God’s Troubadour.

✍️ Writing Practice — answer on paper first (not scored)
1. Imagine you are Peter. Write the body of a short formal letter asking the railway to clear the mound.
👀 Show model answer
Sir, I am writing on behalf of my sisters and myself, who live in the cottage near the railway cutting. This morning a landslide brought rock, earth and trees down onto the line just round the corner from the steps. We were able to stop the 11:29 train in time, but the mound is still blocking the track. I request you to clear it at the earliest, before another train is put in danger. Yours faithfully, Peter.
2. Compare how “Saviours of the Train” and “The Butterfly Mountain’s Shadow” describe nature.
👀 Show model answer
In “Saviours of the Train”, nature is sudden and dangerous: trees “walk” down the cutting and crash onto the line with a blundering roar. In “The Butterfly Mountain’s Shadow”, nature is gentle and wondrous: butterflies flit uphill, the sky becomes a kaleidoscope and a shadow appears like magic. Both writers use vivid sense details to make the scene feel real.
