🌍 Geography · Ch 4 Agriculture · Part 2
Subsistence Farming — Intensive & Shifting
Some farmers grow food only to feed their own families — this is subsistence farming. It has two forms: intensive subsistence farming in crowded monsoon Asia, and primitive subsistence (shifting) farming in tropical forests.

🧭 Two big families of farming

Farming varies with a region's economic development. Developed countries use mechanised farming; under-developed regions still clear forests for cultivation.
• Subsistence farming — grow enough food to feed themselves and their families.
• Commercial farming — grow crops to sell in the market and earn a living.

🌾 Intensive subsistence farming — where?

Practised in densely populated regions, especially the monsoon lands of Asia: India, China, Thailand, Bangladesh and Myanmar.

🧩 Intensive — key features

• Small farms — land is divided among many members of a large family.
• Land is cultivated throughout the year by double cropping, multiple cropping and crop rotation.
• Mostly manual work — fields are too small for machines, labour is plentiful and farmers cannot afford machines.
• Draught animals such as the ox and mule plough fields and carry harvests.
• Food crops: rice, pulses, wheat, millets, oilseeds.

💡 Intensive = maximum output from a small piece of land.

🔥 Primitive subsistence / shifting agriculture

Also called the slash-and-burn method. Practised by forest tribes in the north-eastern hills and Andaman & Nicobar Islands of India, and in the Amazon Basin, Zaire (Congo) Basin, Indonesia and Malaysia.

🪓 How shifting cultivation works

1. Clear a small patch of forest by cutting trees and burning the rest.
2. Mix the ash into the soil to add fertility.
3. Use primitive tools, with no irrigation or fertilisers.
4. When fertility falls after a few years, abandon the plot and clear a fresh one.
Crops: rice, millets, cassava, yam.

💡 It is NOT eco-friendly because it involves deforestation.

🔄 Smart practices of small farmers

Subsistence farmers keep land productive by leaving land fallow (resting), crop rotation, multiple cropping and using manure (animal dung).
Crop rotation: growing the same crop for more than two years exhausts soil; alternate nitrogen-fixing legumes (like soybean, pulses) with soil-exhausting grains such as wheat.

🎯 Must-remember facts
• Subsistence = for own family · Commercial = for market
• Intensive: monsoon Asia, tiny plots, year-round, manual, ox & mule, rice/pulses/wheat/millets/oilseeds
• Shifting = slash-and-burn: NE India, Andaman & Nicobar, Amazon, Zaire, Indonesia, Malaysia
• Shifting crops: rice, millets, cassava, yam · no irrigation, no fertiliser
• Legumes fix nitrogen · wheat exhausts soil

✍️ English Grammar · Ch 2 Determiners
Determiners — some, any, few, little, each, every…
A determiner sits before a noun and tells us which, whose, how many or how much. To pick the right one you first ask: is the noun countable or uncountable?

🧮 Countable vs uncountable

Countable: one boy, two girls, six eggs — have singular and plural forms.
Uncountable: water, air, bread, money, gold, sugar, milk, rice, and abstract nouns like knowledge, beauty, anger, love. They have no plural.
Common uncountables that trick everyone: advice, information, news, furniture, luggage, baggage, accommodation, traffic, progress, weather, work.

💡 ✗ advices, informations, furnitures, luggages — these words never take -s.

📏 Quantity words

Countable only: many, a few, few, several, a number of, fewer.
Uncountable only: much, a little, little, a great deal of, less.
Both: some, any, a lot of, plenty of, all, most, no.

many children · a few teachers · much noise · a little water

🔍 few vs a few · little vs a little

a few / a little = some, enough (positive). few / little = almost none (negative).
• I have a few friends — I am fine.
• He has few friends — he is lonely.
• There is a little milk — enough for tea.
• There is little hope — almost none.

💡 The little word 'a' adds a little hope!

❓ some vs any

Some in positive sentences and in offers/requests: I need some sugar. Would you like some tea?
Any in negatives and most questions: Is there any butter left? I don't have any pens.

👥 Distributives: each, every, either, neither

Each = one by one (2 or more). Every = all of a group (3 or more), never with 'of'.
Either = one of two. Neither = not this one and not that one.
All four take a singular noun and a singular verb: Every man in the room looked at the clock.

✓ each of the boys   ✗ every of the boys

📚 The full family

• Articles: a, an, the
• Demonstratives: this, that, these, those
• Possessives: my, your, his, her, its, our, their
• Numbers: cardinal (one, two) and ordinal (first, last)
• Multipliers: double, twice, three times
• Fractions: half, one-third
• Intensifiers: What! Such! (Such arrogance!)
• Interrogatives: what, whose, which

👉 this/that · these/those

This/these = near; that/those = far. This/that with singular; these/those with plural. ✓ These fruits are not fresh but those are.

📌 Singular nouns always need one

A singular countable noun can never stand alone: ✗ I bought pen. ✓ I bought a pen / my pen / that pen. Plural and uncountable nouns can stand alone: Honesty is the best policy. I have friends.

✨ Fun facts
• Fewer is for things you count, less for things you measure. A shop sign saying '10 items or less' is a famous grammar mistake — it should be '10 items or fewer'.
• The word news looks plural but is singular. Long ago people really wrote 'the news are'.
• 'A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush' uses a determiner (a), a number (two) and the article the in one short proverb.
• Information is uncountable in English but countable in French (des informations) — a common mistake for French learners.
🎓 Trivia — can you answer?
Q. Which is right: 'much people' or 'many people'?
👀 Show answerMany people — people is countable (plural).

Q. 'Few students passed' — good news or bad news?
👀 Show answerBad news. Few = almost none. 'A few' would be more positive.

Q. Can you say 'a luggage'?
👀 Show answerNo. Say 'a piece of luggage' or 'a bag'.
🎯 Must-remember rules
• many / few / a few / fewer + countable; much / little / a little / less + uncountable
• few & little = almost none; a few & a little = some
• some in positives and offers; any in negatives and questions
• each, every, either, neither + singular noun + singular verb
• No plural: advice, information, news, furniture, luggage, equipment
