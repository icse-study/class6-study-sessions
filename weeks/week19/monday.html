📖 English Literature · Week 19 · Revision
Revision · Ch 1–2 — The Most Beautiful Thing, The Sailor, The Little Prince, Rakhi
Four texts, one idea: children see the world differently, and love means taking them seriously. Re-read the cards, then try 20 hard questions — none repeated from Weeks 12–13.
🔁 Quick recap

The Most Beautiful Thing (Rupa Bajwa) — five-year-old Ramchand cannot decide what “the most beautiful thing in the world” is; his parents respond with silence, truth and love.
The Sailor (Tagore) — a child imagines sailing Madhu’s idle jute boat across seven seas and thirteen rivers of fairyland, home by dark.
The Little Prince (Saint-Exupéry) — a pilot who gave up drawing at six crashes in the Sahara and meets a boy who wants a sheep drawn; only a box will do.
Rakhi (Vikram Seth) — a brother far from home ties his sister’s rakhi himself; people change, the custom stays.

🧩 Links between the texts

Childhood vs adulthood: grown-ups who “never understand anything by themselves” (Little Prince) vs parents who do understand (Most Beautiful Thing).
Imagination: a box with a sheep inside; a jute boat that becomes a prince’s ship.
Family bonds: Ramchand and his parents; the brother and sister in “Rakhi”; the sailor reassuring his mother.
Honesty: Pa’s “I don’t know that either”; the pilot’s frankness about grown-ups.

✍️ Writing Practice — answer on paper first (not scored)
1. Compare the grown-ups in “The Little Prince” with Ramchand’s parents.
👀 Show model answer
The grown-ups in “The Little Prince” see a hat instead of a boa digesting an elephant and tell the boy to give up drawing; they cannot enter a child’s imagination. Ramchand’s parents are the opposite: they take his confusing question seriously, do not laugh, and even admit they do not know the answer. One set of adults crushes a child’s spirit; the other makes the child feel loved and understood.
2. How do “The Sailor” and “The Little Prince” celebrate imagination?
👀 Show model answer
In “The Sailor” an idle jute boat becomes a ship with a hundred oars sailing to fairyland. In “The Little Prince” a plain box becomes the perfect sheep. Both show that a child’s imagination can turn ordinary things into wonderful ones, something many grown-ups have forgotten how to do.
