Algebra — Multiplication & Division Practice
Chapter 12. Everything from last week put together: FOIL for binomials, and term-by-term division.
➗ The four rules on one card

Multiplying monomials

Multiply the coefficients, add the exponents.

(3x²)(4x³) = 12x⁵  ·  (−7a²b)(2ab²) = −14a³b³

Multiplying binomials — FOIL

First · Outer · Inner · Last, then collect like terms.

(x + 5)(x + 2)
F: x·x = x²   O: x·2 = 2x   I: 5·x = 5x   L: 5·2 = 10
→ x² + 7x + 10
💡 (3x − 4y)(3x + 4y) = 9x² − 16y² — the O and I terms cancel whenever the brackets are a + and − pair.

Dividing monomials

Divide the coefficients, subtract the exponents.

12x⁶ ÷ 3x² = 4x⁴  ·  (−24a³b²) ÷ (6ab) = −4a²b

Dividing a polynomial by a monomial

Split it up and divide each term on its own — and mind the sign of the divisor.

(20x³y − 15xy³) ÷ 5xy = 4x² − 3y²
(15x⁴ − 20x³) ÷ (−5x²) = −3x² + 4x
Before the quiz
Say it out loud as you work: "multiply → add the powers; divide → subtract the powers." Most wrong answers in this set come from swapping those two.
📜 The Story of Algebra · Part 7 of 16 — FOIL — A Nickname for an Old Rule

Where the word FOIL comes from

FOIL is not an ancient theorem — it is a modern memory aid for multiplying two binomials: First, Outer, Inner, Last. The rule underneath it is far older: every term of one bracket must meet every term of the other.

(x + 3)(x + 5) → x·x + x·5 + 3·x + 3·5 = x² + 8x + 15
💡 FOIL only works for two brackets of two terms. The real rule — multiply each by each — never runs out.

How this looked before symbols

Al-Khwarizmi and his predecessors did these same multiplications, but entirely in sentences, and often by drawing rectangles and cutting them into pieces. The Greek habit of turning algebra into geometry made products visible as areas.

A rectangle (x + 3) by (x + 5) splits into four pieces: x², 5x, 3x and 15 — which is exactly FOIL, drawn.
💡 If you ever forget the middle term of (a + b)², draw the square. The two rectangles of area ab are impossible to miss.

And division is the same rule reversed

Dividing a polynomial by a monomial means dividing every term — coefficients divided, exponents subtracted.

(15x⁴ − 20x³) ÷ (−5x²) = −3x² + 4x   (watch the sign on the second term)
⚗️ Chemistry — Matter: States and Properties (Chapter 3, Part 1)

🧊 What is matter?

Anything that has mass and occupies space is matter. Your book, the water in your glass, and the air in the room are all matter — even though you cannot see the air.

Light, sound and heat are not matter. They have no mass and take up no space.
💡 All matter is built from tiny particles called atoms and molecules.

🧊 The three states, side by side

The difference between the three states is simply how close the particles are and how strongly they pull on each other.

Solid — definite shape, definite volume · particles packed tightly · strongest force · cannot be compressed · does not diffuse
Liquid — definite volume, no definite shape · particles less tightly packed · can flow · diffuses slowly
Gas — no definite shape, no definite volume · particles far apart · very compressible · diffuses very fast
💡 Liquids and gases are both called fluids, for one reason only: they can flow.

⚛️ The four words you must be able to define

These four terms explain every property in the table above.

Intermolecular force — the force of attraction between molecules
Intermolecular space — the gap between molecules
Diffusion — particles of one substance mixing on their own with another
Brownian movement — the zig-zag motion of tiny particles suspended in a liquid or gas
💡 Biggest gaps and weakest force → gas. Smallest gaps and strongest force → solid. Almost every question follows from that one sentence.

Why does a gas fill the whole room?

Because its particles are far apart and moving freely at high speed, a gas has nothing holding it in shape. It spreads until it meets the walls — which is also why gases are easy to compress and diffuse fastest.

Open a bottle of perfume in one corner and the whole room smells of it — that is diffusion in a gas.
🎯 Fun Trivia
The idea of atoms is over 2,500 years old. The Indian philosopher Maharishi Kanad proposed that matter is made of indivisible particles long before any instrument could see one, and John Dalton turned the idea into modern atomic theory in the 1800s. Nobody actually saw an atom until the twentieth century.

Le pluriel des noms · Les verbes en « ir »
Textbook pages 26–29. How nouns change in the plural, the exceptions you must memorise, and the second group of verbs.
📏 The general rule

Add an s — but you don't hear it

Most nouns add -s in the plural. The s is silent, so the plural sounds the same — the article (les / des) tells you it is plural.

le garçon → les garçons · le bonbon → les bonbons · la glace → les glaces · l'homme → les hommes

🔧 Second group — verbs ending in -ir

Remove the r to get the radical (finir → fini), then add -s, -s, -t, -ssons, -ssez, -ssent.

🎯 Fun Trivia
French children learn the seven -ou words as a chanted list — bijou, caillou, chou, genou, hibou, joujou, pou — because there is no rule for them; you just have to know them.

The textbook poem « Les pluriels étranges » has them all: le cheveu devient des cheveux, le travail devient les travaux, un gâteau → des gâteaux, mais un pneu est toujours des pneus !
