🗣️ ಕನ್ನಡ ಸಾಹಿತ್ಯ · Cultural & Historical Context
ಹುಬ್ಬಳ್ಳಿ ಸಿದ್ಧಾರೂಢ ಸ್ವಾಮಿ ಜಾತ್ರೆ — The Siddharoodha Swamy Fair
This passage shows how Indian fairs (ಜಾತ್ರೆಗಳು) are not only religious events but also socio-economic unifiers — they give livelihood to artisans, teach culture to children, and strengthen communal harmony. Read these cards, try the Writing Practice, then take the quiz.
🏛️ Core Theme & History

Fairs as socio-economic unifiers

The passage highlights that fairs (ಜಾತ್ರೆಗಳು) are far more than religious gatherings. They provide livelihood to hundreds of artisans (ಕಸಬುದಾರರು), instil cultural values in children, and strengthen communal harmony (ಒಗ್ಗಟ್ಟು).

💡 Three gifts of a fair: jobs · culture · unity

The Math & the Freedom Struggle

The Siddharoodha Swamy Math (ಮಠ) in Hubballi is portrayed as a spiritual fortress that was active during the Indian freedom struggle. The teacher connects it to national leaders like Gandhi and Tilak — showing that spiritual institutions often doubled as hubs of political awakening.

Idea: ಮಠ = spiritual centre + centre of national inspiration during ಸ್ವಾತಂತ್ರ್ಯ ಹೋರಾಟ.
✨ Literary Imagery (Slogans)

"ಸಿದ್ಧಾರೂಢರ ಅಂಗಾರ, ದೇಶಕ್ಕೆಲ್ಲ ಬಂಗಾರ"

Siddharoodha's spark is gold for the nation. The seer's small spark of inspiration (ಅಂಗಾರ → spark / ember) is precious like ಬಂಗಾರ → gold for the whole country.

💡 Metaphor: a tiny ember → priceless gold for the nation

"ಸಿದ್ಧಾರೂಢರ ಜೋಳಿಗೆ, ನಾಡಿಗೆಲ್ಲ ಹೋಳಿಗೆ"

Siddharoodha's sack is a sweet dish for the land. The seer's begging sack (ಜೋಳಿಗೆ → sack) becomes a sweet ಹೋಳಿಗೆ → holige (sweet) for everyone — he gives more than he takes. These metaphors elevate the seer to a national benefactor.

📖 Key Vocabulary (Literature)

Important words

ಹಿರೇಮಣಿ — leader / senior of a group (literally 'big gem').
ಕರ್ಮಭೂಮಿ — land of action / duty (opposite of ಭೋಗಭೂಮಿ, land of pleasure).
ಜನಸ್ಥೋಮ — a massive crowd / sea of people.

ಅಂಗಾರ = spark/ember · ಬಂಗಾರ = gold · ಜೋಳಿಗೆ = sack · ಹೋಳಿಗೆ = sweet (holige)
💡 Exam Tip — Remember the two slogans
ಅಂಗಾರ → ಬಂಗಾರ (spark → gold)
ಜೋಳಿಗೆ → ಹೋಳಿಗೆ (sack → sweet)
Both elevate the seer to a national benefactor.

📖 English Literature · Day 1 · R.K. Narayan
"When Granny Wanted a Lemon" — Part 1
From Malgudi Schooldays. Swami’s pity, his temper, Granny’s request for a lemon — and his refusal. Read the cards, try the Writing Practice, then take the quiz.
📖 The Story — Part 1

How the story opens

One evening, returning from the cricket field after parting from his friend Mani at the Grove Street junction, Swami (Swaminathan) finds his conscience troubling him. The story then goes back to what happened earlier that evening.

Coffee, pity — and temper

Home from school to keep his books, Swami is running to the kitchen when Granny calls, “Swami, Swami.” He says “No,” as usual, and violently sucks coffee out of a tumbler. Her shaky, querulous voice gives him a fit of pity — she sits calling people who pay no heed to her. He goes to her: “What do you want?”

She asks him to sit down — a careful, diplomatic first move, because she knows what to expect. At that he loses his temper: “say it as quickly as possible… don’t think I am a silly fool.”

The offer — and the refusal

Granny has a terrible stomach ache. She offers six pies:

3 pies → for Swami to keep  ·  3 pies → to buy a lemon.

“Without condescending to say yes or no”, he takes the coins. But when she says, “You must come before I count ten”, the time limit irritates him. He throws down the coins: “If you want it so urgently, you had better go and get it yourself.” It is nearly five-thirty and he wants to reach the field before sunset. She pleads that her stomach hurts terribly — he does not stay to hear more.

📒 Vocabulary

Key words

Querulous — complaining in a whining manner.
Paid no heed — gave no attention.
Diplomatically — carefully and sensitively.
Condescending — showing an attitude of superiority.
Imposition — an unreasonable demand one is expected to accept.
Pie — a coin used in India until 1947, one-third of a paisa (12 pies = 1 anna) → the story is set in pre-independence India.

✍️ Writing Practice — answer on paper, then check
1. Swami feels “a fit of pity” for Granny. What causes it, and what does it show about the household?
▸ Show model answer
He hears her weak, querulous voice calling people who pay no heed to her. It shows that the family ignores the old woman, and that Swami, for a moment at least, notices her loneliness.
2. Why did Granny ask Swami to sit down before making her request?
▸ Show model answer
It was her first diplomatic move. She knew her grandson was impatient and often said “No”, so she wanted to open the question slowly and carefully.
3. What made Swami throw down the coins?
▸ Show model answer
The time limit — “You must come before I count ten.” He felt it was an imposition, and he was in a hurry to reach the cricket field before sunset.
4. How does Swami’s behaviour change within a few minutes?
▸ Show model answer
He moves from refusing (“No,” as usual) to pity for Granny, then to anger when asked to sit, then to rudeness when he throws down the coins. His moods change quickly — he is impulsive.
5. What does “pies” tell us about the setting?
▸ Show model answer
Pies were small coins used in India until 1947, so the story is set in pre-independence India, in Narayan’s town of Malgudi.
