🌍 Geography · Ch 4 Agriculture · Part 5
The Green Revolution — Boon or Bane?
In the 1960s many developing countries faced hunger. A new way of farming — the Green Revolution — made harvests boom. But it also brought problems. This week you weigh both sides.

🌱 What was it?

The Green Revolution means large-scale production of crops. It was born out of the need to solve poverty and hunger in developing countries in the 1960s.

👨‍🔬 Who and where?

American scientist Norman Ernest Borlaug introduced it. It was tried first on wheat, later on rice. In India, Punjab was the first state to adopt it in wheat farms.

💡 Borlaug won the Nobel Peace Prize (1970) for this work.

🧰 Five inputs

1. High Yield Variety (HYV) seeds
2. Chemical fertilisers
3. Pesticides
4. Irrigation
5. Modern machinery

✅ Benefits

• HYV seeds increased production.
• Fertilisers increased grain size.
• Pesticides prevented crop loss.
• Irrigation gave water even with little or no rain.
• Machines made farm work faster.

❌ Drawbacks

• Only rich farmers with large farms gained; small farmers could not afford inputs.
• Machines caused severe unemployment.
• Overuse of chemicals damaged soil quality.
• Groundwater depleted by overuse for irrigation.
• Production rose for only specific grains (wheat, rice); other crops unaffected.

📈 The yield graph

Your textbook graph (Fig 4.3) shows yields per hectare of Mexico, India and Pakistan from 1950 to 2000 rising sharply after the 1960s — the Green Revolution effect.

♻️ The way forward

We need sustainable farming that keeps soil fertile, water available and farmers' income steady: multiple cropping, crop rotation and sprinkler irrigation (also drip irrigation).

🎯 Must-remember facts
• 1960s · Norman Borlaug · wheat first, then rice · Punjab first in India
• HYV seeds, fertilisers, pesticides, irrigation, machinery
• Drawbacks: rich-only, unemployment, soil damage, groundwater loss, only wheat & rice
• Sustainable: multiple cropping, crop rotation, sprinkler irrigation

✍️ English Grammar · Ch 5 Subject-Verb Agreement
Subject–Verb Agreement — is or are? has or have?
Singular subject → singular verb. Plural subject → plural verb. Easy? The trick is finding the real subject. Phrases like one of, along with, a pair of and words like news and mathematics try to fool you.

🎯 Find the real subject

Ignore the words in between.
• The price of these jeans is reasonable. (subject = price)
• The books borrowed from the library are on my desk.
• The boy who won the two medals is my friend.
• One of the students plays hockey. (subject = one)

💡 One of + plural noun → singular verb.

➕ Joined by AND

Two subjects with and → plural: John and Mary have come.
BUT one idea or one person → singular:
• Bread and butter is our breakfast.
• The famous singer and composer has arrived. (one person — only one 'the')
• Every man, woman and child attends. (every → singular)

📍 Rule of proximity

With either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also, the verb agrees with the subject nearest to it.
• Either the employees or their manager is to blame.
• Either her brother or her parents have her address.
But as well as, along with, with, together with do NOT change the subject:
• My father, as well as his colleagues, was robbed.
• The boys, along with the dog, are going.

1️⃣ Always singular

• each, every, everyone, everybody, nobody, somebody, anybody, either of, neither of, none of, every one of
• news; diseases & subjects in -s: measles, mumps, mathematics, physics, economics, athletics
• class words: furniture, stationery, crockery, cutlery, clothing, footwear
• a pair of scissors/shoes/trousers (but The scissors are)
• amounts taken as a whole: Ten kilometres is; Ten thousand rupees is; Thirty years is
• collective nouns as one unit: A flock of sheep was; A gang of thieves was
• titles: The Arabian Nights is; Many a student has

👥 Always plural

people, cattle, police, a dozen, a number of, the rich/the poor, both, several, many, few
• A number of accidents have happened. (but The number of accidents is rising.)

⚖️ Depends on the noun after OF

some of, half of, most of, all of, a lot of, plenty of — countable plural → plural; uncountable → singular.
• Half of the eggs were rotten. · Half of the land was barren.
• Most of the money is in the safe. · A lot of people were there.

🚪 There is / there are

With there, look at the noun that follows: There was an accident. There are forty students. There, in the aisle, was my cart along with all the items.

✨ Fun facts
• Measles and mumps look plural because they once meant many spots (measles) and 'grimaces' (mumps), but they are singular diseases today.
• In British English, 'The team are playing well' is often accepted (thinking of members). In Indian exams, treat a collective noun acting as one unit as singular.
• 'The jury are divided in their opinion' — when members act separately, the collective noun takes a plural verb.
• The Arabian Nights has over 1,000 years of history and contains stories like Aladdin and Sinbad.
🎓 Trivia — can you answer?
Q. Is it 'Mathematics is' or 'Mathematics are'?
👀 Show answerMathematics is — subject names in -s are singular.

Q. 'Neither the teacher nor the students ___ ready.'
👀 Show answerare — nearest subject (students) is plural.

Q. 'A number of' vs 'The number of' — which takes 'is'?
👀 Show answerThe number of … is. A number of … are.
🎯 Must-remember rules
• one of + plural noun → singular verb
• either/or, neither/nor, not only/but also → verb agrees with NEAREST subject
• as well as / along with / with → verb agrees with FIRST subject
• news, mathematics, measles, furniture, each, every, none of → singular
• people, cattle, police, a number of, the rich → plural
