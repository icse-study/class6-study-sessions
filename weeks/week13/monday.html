Circles — Centre, Radius & Diameter
Chapter 17 · Sections 17.1–17.3. What a circle really is, and the three words everything else is built on.
➗ The ideas

What a circle is

A circle is a closed curve on which every point is the same distance from one fixed point inside it.

Another way to say it: a circle is the path traced by a moving point that always stays at a fixed distance from a fixed point.

If O is the centre and A, B, C, D are on the circle, then OA = OB = OC = OD.
💡 The boundary itself is the circumference. The fixed point is the centre. The fixed distance is the radius.

Radius

The radius is the line segment joining the centre to any point on the circumference — and also the length of that segment.

A circle has countless radii (that is the plural of radius), and every one of them is the same length.

OP = OA = OB = radius

Diameter

A diameter is a straight line that passes through the centre with both ends on the circumference.

Diameter = 2 × Radius  ·  Radius = Diameter ÷ 2
💡 A diameter cuts the circle into two equal parts, and each part is called a semi-circle.

Where does a point lie?

Take a circle with centre O and radius r, and any point P in the same plane. Compare the distance OP with r:

OP > r → P lies outside the circle
OP = r → P lies on the circle (on the circumference)
OP < r → P lies inside the circle
💡 The centre O is always inside — its distance from itself is 0, which is less than r.
🎉 Fun facts & trivia

A circle is the only shape that looks exactly the same after you turn it by any angle. A square needs a quarter turn, an equilateral triangle a third of a turn — a circle needs nothing at all.

Manhole covers are round for a mathematical reason: a circle has the same width in every direction, so a round cover can never fall through its own hole. A square cover can — down the diagonal.

Of all the shapes with the same perimeter, the circle encloses the largest area. That is why bubbles are round: the soap film pulls itself into the shape that holds the most air for the least skin.

📜 Part 1 of 12 — The Wheel Came Late

Humans farmed, built cities and wrote poetry before anyone invented the wheel. The oldest known wheels — from around 3500 BCE in Mesopotamia — were not for carts at all, but potters' wheels for shaping clay.

The hard part was never the round shape. It was the axle. A wheel only rolls smoothly if every point on its rim stays exactly the same distance from the centre — which is precisely the definition you learnt today. Get that distance wrong by even a little, and the cart bumps along.

So the first engineers were, without knowing the word, testing whether their radius was constant.
