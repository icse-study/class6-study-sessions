Revision 1 — Numbers, Factors & Fractions
Weeks 1–7 revision. Place value, divisibility, HCF and LCM, fractions and decimals — the foundation everything else stands on.
➗ The ideas

Place value, Indian system

Indian system: ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten thousands, lakhs, ten lakhs, crores.
Face value is the digit itself; place value is the digit × its place.

In 4,72,153 the digit 7 has face value 7 and place value 70,000.
1 lakh = 1,00,000 (5 zeros) · 1 crore = 1,00,00,000 (7 zeros)
💡 Rounding: look at the digit to the right of the rounding place. 5 or more rounds up, 4 or less stays.

Divisibility tests

2 — last digit even · 3 — digit sum divisible by 3 · 4 — last two digits divisible by 4 · 5 — ends in 0 or 5 · 6 — divisible by 2 and 3 · 9 — digit sum divisible by 9 · 10 — ends in 0 · 11 — difference of alternate digit sums is 0 or a multiple of 11.

5,436 → last two digits 36, divisible by 4 → yes.

HCF and LCM

HCF — the largest number that divides all of them. LCM — the smallest number they all divide into.

For two numbers: HCF × LCM = product of the numbers.
HCF of 12 and 18 = 6 · LCM of 12 and 18 = 36 · 6 × 36 = 216 = 12 × 18
💡 Word problems: 'greatest length / largest group' → HCF. 'ring together again / smallest number of' → LCM.

Fractions and decimals

Proper (numerator smaller), improper (numerator larger), mixed. Equivalent fractions come from multiplying or dividing both parts by the same number.

To compare fractions, make the denominators the same.
7/8 = 0.875 · 3/4 = 0.75 · 2/5 = 0.4
💡 A fraction is a division waiting to happen: 7/8 means 7 ÷ 8.
🎉 Fun facts & trivia

The digits 0–9 you write were invented in India and reached Europe through Arabic scholars — which is why they are called Hindu-Arabic numerals. Try doing long division in Roman numerals and you will see why they won.

Zero as a number in its own right was first written down clearly by Brahmagupta in 628 CE, along with the rules for adding and subtracting with it.

A lakh is 10⁵ and a crore is 10⁷. The Indian comma system groups digits as 1,00,00,000, unlike the international 10,000,000.

📜 The Year in Numbers · Part 1 of 4 — Why We Count in Tens

There is no mathematical reason to count in tens. Base 10 is an accident of anatomy: we have ten fingers.

Other civilisations chose differently. The Babylonians used base 60, which is why an hour has 60 minutes and a circle 360 degrees. The Mayans used base 20, counting fingers and toes. Computers use base 2, because a switch is either off or on.

What makes our system powerful is not the ten — it is place value plus zero. The same ten symbols can write any number ever needed, simply by moving them left.
