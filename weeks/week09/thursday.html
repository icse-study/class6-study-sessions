Algebra — Introduction
Chapter 12 · Sections 12.1–12.5. The vocabulary of algebra: constants, variables, terms, like and unlike, and the names for expressions.
➗ The building blocks

What algebra is

Algebra is generalised arithmetic — the same rules of number work, but with letters standing in for numbers so one statement can cover every case at once.

Arithmetic: 2 + 3 = 3 + 2  ·  Algebra: a + b = b + a  (true for all numbers)

Constants and variables

Constant: a symbol with a fixed value — 5, −7, ¾.
Variable (literal): a symbol whose value can change — x, y, a, b.

In 3x + 5: 3 is the coefficient, x is the variable, 5 is a constant.

Terms and coefficients

A term is a constant, a variable, or a product of them: 5x, −3, xy, 7x²y.

The coefficient of a part of a term is everything else multiplied with it:

In 7x²y → coefficient of x² is 7y; coefficient of y is 7x²
In −3xy → coefficient of x is −3y; coefficient of xy is −3

Like and unlike terms

Like terms have exactly the same variables raised to exactly the same powers: 3x and −5x; 2xy and 7xy; −2ab and 5ba (order doesn't matter).

Unlike terms differ in variable or in power: 3x and 3y; 2x² and 2x; 4x and 5y².

💡 Only like terms can be added or subtracted into a single term. 2a + 3b has to stay as it is.

Degree, and what counts as a polynomial

The degree of a polynomial in one variable is the highest power of that variable: 5x³ − 2x + 7 has degree 3.

For a single term with several variables, the degree is the sum of the exponents: 3xy²z³ → 1 + 2 + 3 = 6.

💡 An expression is not a polynomial if a variable sits in a denominator or under a root — 5x + 1/x fails the test.
Before the quiz
When a question asks for "the coefficient of x", cover x with your thumb — whatever is left in that term is the answer, sign included.

📜 The Story of Algebra · Part 3 of 16 — The Word 'Algebra' Is Born

🇮🇷 Al-Khwarizmi, about 820 CE

The Persian mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi wrote a book that changed mathematics. In its title was the Arabic word al-jabr, meaning restoring or recombining parts — what you do when you move a term to the other side to tidy up an equation.

al-jabr → algebra
💡 The same man gave us a second word. al-Khwarizmi → algorithm. One mathematician named both algebra and the algorithms that run every computer you own.

🇬🇷 Greece and Diophantus

Earlier, the Greeks had linked algebra with geometry — drawing shapes to represent relationships between quantities. In the 3rd century CE Diophantus wrote Arithmetica, a whole book of problems about unknowns, and is often called the father of algebra.

Al-Khwarizmi is also often given that title — both claims are fair, for different reasons.

Why this matters for today's lesson

The vocabulary you are learning — variable, constant, term, coefficient, degree — is the finished product of that long journey. Each word exists so a whole sentence of description can be replaced by one short symbol.

“three times the square of an unknown number”  →  3x²
⚗️ Chemistry — The Chemistry Laboratory (Chapter 2)

🧪 What a laboratory is for

A chemistry laboratory is a place to perform experiments, observe chemical processes and analyse the results. Every rule and every piece of apparatus in it exists for one of two reasons: to make the experiment work, or to keep you safe.

Science stops being something you read about and becomes something you do.

🔬 The apparatus and what each one does

Learn these by their job, not just their shape — that is how questions are asked.

Test tube — small tests and heating
Beaker — prepare and hold solutions
Conical flask — hold liquids during experiments
Measuring cylinder — measure liquid volume accurately
Funnel — filtering and transferring liquids
Evaporating dish — evaporate liquids (made of porcelain)
Mortar and pestle — grind solids into powder
Wire gauze — sits on a tripod to spread heat evenly
Bunsen burner / spirit lamp — heating
Reagent bottle — store chemicals safely
Gas jar — collect gases
Test tube holder — hold a test tube while heating
💡 Beaker vs measuring cylinder is a favourite trap. A beaker only holds; a measuring cylinder measures accurately.

🧠 Why is nearly everything made of glass?

Four reasons, and you should be able to give all four:

1. Glass is transparent — you can watch the reaction happen.
2. Glass withstands high temperatures (especially Pyrex / Borosil).
3. Glass is easy to clean.
4. Glass does not react with most chemicals.
💡 Pyrex is used because it expands very little when heated, so it does not crack when a hot tube meets cold air.

⚠️ Safety rules that are never optional

Every one of these has been written because somebody once got hurt.

• Always wear a lab coat
• Never touch or taste an unknown chemical
• Never point the mouth of a test tube at anyone while heating
• Wash your hands after every experiment
• Report any accident to your teacher immediately
💡 If a chemical spills on your skin: wash at once under cold running water, then tell your teacher. Do not wipe it, and do not put cream on it.
🎯 Fun Trivia
Robert William Boyle (1627–1691) was the first scientist to write down the apparatus, the procedure and the observations for every experiment he did — so that others could repeat it and check. That single habit is why he is called the father of modern chemistry, and it is exactly what your practical record book is for.
