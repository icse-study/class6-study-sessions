Substitution — Putting Numbers Back In
Chapter 13 · An expression is a rule waiting for numbers. Substitute, then follow BODMAS — carefully, especially with negative values.
➗ What substitution means

Replace the letter, keep the operation

To evaluate an expression, replace every letter with the number given and work out the value. The letter is only a placeholder.

If x = 4:   x + 7 = 4 + 7 = 11  ·  5x = 5 × 4 = 20  ·  x² = 4 × 4 = 16
💡 5x means 5 × x. Once you substitute, put the multiplication sign back in — writing 5x = 54 when x = 4 is the most common slip of all.

Brackets keep you safe

When you substitute a negative number, put it in brackets first, then simplify.

If a = −3:   2a = 2 × (−3) = −6  ·  a² = (−3) × (−3) = 9  ·  −a = −(−3) = 3
💡 a² is always positive (or zero), but −a² means −(a²). If a = −3, then −a² = −9, not +9.
➗ Order of operations

BODMAS after substituting

Substitute first, then Brackets → Of/Orders → Division → Multiplication → Addition → Subtraction.

If x = 5, y = 2:   3x + 4y = 15 + 8 = 23  ·  3(x + y) = 3 × 7 = 21  ·  x² − y³ = 25 − 8 = 17

Two letters, one expression

Substitute every letter before doing any arithmetic — never halfway through.

If p = 6, q = 3:   pq = 18  ·  p ÷ q = 2  ·  p² − 2q = 36 − 6 = 30  ·  (p − q)² = 3² = 9
💡 (p − q)² and p² − q² are different: 9 versus 27. Brackets change everything.
➗ Using formulas

Formulas are expressions with names

Perimeter, area and average are all substitution problems in disguise.

Rectangle: P = 2(l + b). If l = 9 cm, b = 4 cm → P = 2 × 13 = 26 cm, A = l × b = 36 cm²
💡 Always write the formula, then the substitution, then the answer with its unit. Three lines earn full marks in an ICSE paper.

📜 The Story of Algebra · Part 10 of 16 — What Substitution Really Means

The letter is a place, not a thing

A variable is a waiting space. When you substitute, you fill the space and the expression collapses into a single number. 3x + 2 is a machine; x = 4 is what you feed it; 14 is what comes out.

x = 4 → 3(4) + 2 = 14  ·  x = 10 → 3(10) + 2 = 32  ·  one machine, endless answers

Why x, of all letters?

There is no single agreed reason. Descartes used letters from the end of the alphabet for unknowns, printers had plenty of spare x blocks, and the habit stuck. x has no special power — any letter would do the same job.

💡 In physics you will meet t for time, v for velocity and m for mass. Same idea, friendlier letters.

Substitution is how formulas become useful

Every formula you will ever meet is an expression waiting for values. Area, perimeter, speed, interest, gravity — all of them are substitution problems once you know the rule.

A = l × b  ·  P = 2(l + b)  ·  A = ½ × b × h  ·  F = ma
💡 Substitute with brackets around negative values — (−3)² = 9, but −3² = −9. The brackets are not optional.

1️⃣ What is matter?

Anything that occupies space and has mass

Books, water, air, your own body — all matter. Air is invisible but still matter: blow into a balloon and it swells, because air occupies space.

💡 Light, heat and sound are NOT matter — they don't occupy space or have mass.

🇮🇳 An idea from ancient India

Maharishi Kanad was the first to suggest that matter is made of extremely tiny particles he called 'anu', made of even smaller 'parmanu'.

2️⃣ Molecules — tiny particles

Molecule

The smallest particle of matter that can exist freely in nature. Its size is about 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹⁰ m — far too small to see.

3️⃣ Space and force between molecules

Inter-molecular space

The gap between molecules. Smallest in solids, larger in liquids, largest in gases.

Inter-molecular force

The pull between molecules. Strongest in solids, weaker in liquids, weakest (almost nil) in gases.

💡 Big spaces + weak forces = easy to squeeze and quick to spread. That's why gases compress and diffuse easily.
ForceBetweenExample
Cohesive forcemolecules of the SAME substancewater–water, iron–iron
Adhesive forcemolecules of DIFFERENT substanceswater–glass, paint–wall, ink–paper

Why water wets glass but mercury doesn't

Water: adhesion (water–glass) > cohesion → it sticks and wets the glass.
Mercury: adhesion (mercury–glass) < cohesion → it rolls off in drops.

Some special facts

• Surface tension pulls a liquid's surface inwards, so drops are round.
• Gas pressure comes from molecules hitting the walls of the container.
• Expansion on heating: gases > liquids > solids.
• Diffusion is fastest in gases, slower in liquids, slowest in solids.
• Plasma, the 4th state, exists at very high temperatures — like in the Sun.

💡 A rubber band changes shape but is still a solid. A sponge squashes because its pores hold air — the solid itself isn't compressed.

Heating and cooling

Heat a solid → molecules move faster, spaces grow → it melts, then boils.
Cool a gas → molecules slow down and come closer → it condenses, then freezes.

🎯 Fun Trivia
• A single drop of water contains about 1.5 sextillion (1,500,000,000,000,000,000,000) molecules.
• Glass looks solid, and it is — its molecules are just arranged randomly, like a frozen liquid.
• Water is unusual: it expands when it freezes. That's why ice floats and why a full bottle in the freezer can crack.
