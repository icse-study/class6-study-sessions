Ch 10 · Language of The Happy Prince — and Wilde’s two tales
Binomial expressions, direct and indirect speech, the colon, stress shift and describing a process — plus a comparison of “The Happy Prince” with “The Selfish Giant”.
🔗 Binomial expressions

Two words joined by and, or or by, in a fixed order, with a special meaning: little by little (slowly, in stages) · bits and pieces · off and on (sometimes) · more or less (almost) · sooner or later · step by step · far and wide (over a large area) · neat and tidy · short and sweet · make or break · win or lose · by and large (generally) · give or take (approximately) · spick and span (very clean) · part and parcel (an essential part) · hustle and bustle.

🗣️ Direct and indirect speech

Direct: the exact words, in quotation marks — The Happy Prince said, “The writer is hungry and cold.”
Indirect (reported): no quotation marks, usually that — The Happy Prince told the swallow that the writer was hungry and cold.
Reporting verb in the present (says, tells) or a universal truth → no tense change.
Reporting verb in the past → tenses step back: is → was · am going → was going · has seen → had seen · saw → had seen · will → would · can → could · may → might.
Pronouns change (I → he/she, we → they); time and place change: now → then, today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, yesterday → the previous day, here → there, this → that, tonight → that night, ago → before.

✏️ Colon, stress shift, process

Colon (:) after a speaker’s name in a play · before a list · before an explanation · in time (10:30 a.m.).
Stress shift: GEN/er/ous → gen/e/ROS/i/ty · ad/MIRE → AD/mir/a/ble → ad/mi/RA/tion · PHO/to/graph → pho/TOG/ra/pher · com/PETE → com/pe/TI/tion.
Describing a process: steps in order with linkers — first, second, next, then, after that, finally.

🌹 Wilde’s two tales compared

The Selfish Giant (Week 6): a giant shuts children out, his garden freezes in endless winter, he learns to love, and a Christ-like child takes him to Paradise.
The Happy Prince: a statue gives away everything to the poor, and an Angel carries his heart and the swallow to God.
Shared ideas: selfishness versus love · sacrifice · a heavenly reward at the end · fairy tales with a deeper (allegorical) meaning.

✍️ Meet the authors — fun facts & other works

✍️ Oscar Wilde (1854–1900)

Irish playwright and poet, famous for his sharp wit and brilliant conversation.

🌟 Fun facts
• “The Happy Prince” comes from The Happy Prince and Other Tales (1888) — the same collection as “The Selfish Giant” you read in Week 6!
• In Wilde’s original story the Prince has a heart of lead, which snaps in two when the swallow dies; the play changes it to a gold heart.
• In the original, the swallow had stayed behind because he fell in love with a Reed.
• His only novel is The Picture of Dorian Gray; his most famous play is The Importance of Being Earnest.
• Other works: the essay “The Soul of Man under Socialism” and the long poem The Ballad of Reading Gaol.
• He had two sons, Cyril and Vyvyan.

1. Write a short paragraph describing how to make a vegetable sandwich. Use linkers.
👀 Show model answer
First, take two slices of bread and spread butter on one side of each. Next, wash and slice a tomato, a cucumber and an onion. Then, arrange the slices on one piece of bread and sprinkle a little salt and pepper. After that, cover it with the second slice. Finally, cut the sandwich into two triangles and serve.
2. Compare the Happy Prince with the Selfish Giant.
👀 Show model answer
At first they seem opposite: the Giant is selfish and shuts children out, while the Prince wants only to give to the poor. But both stories are about the power of love. The Giant changes from selfishness to love; the Prince shows love through sacrifice. Both end with a heavenly reward — Paradise for the Giant, and God’s garden for the Prince’s heart and the swallow.
