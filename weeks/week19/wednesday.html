🌍 Geography · Ch 5 Minerals · Part 5
Methods of Mining, Hazards & Conservation
How do we get minerals out of the ground — and at what cost? Learn opencast, shaft and alluvial mining, the hazards of mining, and how to conserve minerals.

⛏️ Mining

Mining means extraction of useful minerals from the Earth.

🕳️ 1. Opencast mining

A surface method for minerals close to the surface. The top layer of rock and soil is stripped, and the deposit is mined by strip-mining or the open pit method. Manganese, copper and bauxite are mined mostly this way.

🛗 2. Shaft mining

A large-scale underground method for minerals at great depths, like coal. A long vertical tunnel (shaft) is dug; miners go down in lift cages on cables and pulleys to reach the seams and bring minerals up.

🌊 3. Alluvial mining

Minerals like gold and tin are recovered by sifting mud, sand and gravel. A lot of water washes away lighter particles, leaving heavier minerals behind. A small-scale operation.

💡 Heavy minerals sink; light sand washes away — like panning for gold.

⚠️ Hazards of mining

Mining damages the environment by polluting air, water and soil, and threatens health.
• Mine dust pollutes air; breathing it causes respiratory diseases.
• Waste from mines pollutes rivers and groundwater.
• Forests are cleared, harming biodiversity; land is left scarred.
• Underground mines risk collapse, flooding, fires and poisonous gases.

♻️ Conserving minerals

• Use minerals judiciously and avoid wastage.
• Recycle metals (iron, aluminium, copper).
• Use substitutes and renewable energy in place of fossil fuels.
• Use better technology to mine low-grade ores and cut waste.
• Reclaim and replant mined land.

🎯 Must-remember facts
• Opencast = surface, strip/open pit · manganese, copper, bauxite
• Shaft = underground, deep · coal · lift cages
• Alluvial = sift with water · gold, tin · small scale
• Hazards: dust → lung disease; air, water, soil pollution
• Conserve: judicious use, recycle, substitutes, renewables

✍️ English Grammar · Ch 3–4 Adjectives & Degrees of Comparison (second pass)
Adjectives — elder or older? farther or further?
You know positive, comparative and superlative. Now the traps: irregular forms with two meanings (elder/older, farther/further, later/latter), the five words that take to instead of than, fewer vs less, and changing degrees without changing meaning.

🧩 Kinds of adjectives (recap)

• Quality: a brave girl · Quantity: some rice · Number: three boys, first prize
• Demonstrative: this pen · Distributive: each player · Interrogative: which bus?
• Possessive: my bag · Proper: Indian tea · Emphatic: my own house, the very man
• Exclamatory: What a view!

✍️ Spelling rules

• short word, one vowel + one consonant → double it: big → bigger, thin → thinnest
• consonant + y → i: busy → busier, dry → driest
• long words → more / most: more famous, most difficult
• never both: more faster ✗, most cheapest ✗

🔀 Same word, two meanings

• older (age, takes than) · elder (family, no than): My elder brother is three years older than me.
• farther (distance) · further (more / extra): The shop is farther away. Send further details.
• later / latest (time) · latter / last (order): the latest news; Ravi and Arun came — the latter (Arun) wore red.
• ill → worse → worst · little → less → least

💡 Elder can never be followed by 'than'.

➡️ Five words that take 'to'

superior, inferior, senior, junior, prior — and the verb prefer — take to, never than.
She is senior to me. This cloth is inferior to that one. I prefer tea to coffee.

🔢 fewer or less?

• fewer + countable: fewer people, fewer choices
• less + uncountable: less noise, less sugar
From your book: The takeaway menu has fewer choices than the dine-in menu.

🔄 Changing degree, same meaning

• S: Gold is one of the most precious metals.
• C: Gold is more precious than most other metals.
• P: Very few metals are as precious as gold.
Comparing within a group? Add any other: Everest is higher than any other peak.
not so … as = not as … as: Silver is not so costly as gold.

💡 'one of the' + superlative → 'very few … as … as'.
He is as good as, if not better than, Suresh. (both as and than are needed)
✨ Fun facts
• Mark Twain wrote in a letter to a schoolboy: 'When you catch an adjective, kill it.' He meant: don't stuff sentences with too many.
• Words like unique, perfect, complete are called 'absolute' adjectives — something is either unique or not, so careful writers avoid 'more unique'.
• Good → better → best come from completely different old roots. That's why they look nothing alike.
• 'Funner' and 'funnest' are used in casual speech, but in exams write more fun and most fun.

🎯 Must-remember rules
• elder/eldest = family, no 'than' · older = age, with 'than'
• farther = distance · further = more, extra
• later/latest = time · latter/last = order
• superior, inferior, senior, junior, prior, prefer + to
• fewer + countable · less + uncountable
• 'one of the' + superlative + plural noun
