The Royal Family of Gases — Who They Are and How They Were Found
New topic: the Noble Gases. Day 1 meets the family; Day 2 tells how scientists found them. 30–45 minutes. Then try the 25-question quiz.

🏫 Day 1 — The Secret Society

Think of the Periodic Table as a giant school. Most elements are very social: they make friends (bond) by sharing or swapping electrons. The noble gases are the “cool kids” at the back of the class — their outer shell of electrons is already full, so they are complete and happy and do not need to react. Like nobles who did not mix with common people, they keep to themselves.

💡 Most noble gases have 8 electrons in their outer shell (an octet). Helium is the exception: its only shell is full with just 2.

🔑 Key properties

• Inert — very unreactive
• Colourless, odourless and tasteless
• All are gases at room temperature
• They exist as single atoms (monatomic) — unlike oxygen (O2) or nitrogen (N2)
• They sit in Group 18, the last column of the Periodic Table (older name: Group 0, because their valency is zero)

🔍 Day 2 — The Story of Argon (1894)

Lord Rayleigh noticed that nitrogen taken from air was slightly heavier than nitrogen made in the lab from chemicals. He did not ignore the tiny difference. With William Ramsay, he removed all the oxygen and nitrogen from a sample of air — and a small bubble of an unknown gas was left. They called it argon, from the Greek argos, “lazy”, because it would not react with anything.

💡 Lesson: careful measuring matters. A tiny difference led to a whole new family of elements.

☀️ The Story of Helium (1868)

During a solar eclipse, Pierre Janssen and Norman Lockyer studied the Sun’s light and saw a mysterious yellow line that matched no known element. Lockyer named it helium, after Helios, the Greek Sun god. Helium was found in the Sun before it was found on Earth — Ramsay found it on Earth only in 1895.

🧪 The rest of the family

In 1898 Ramsay and Morris Travers found three more in liquid air: neon (Greek neos, “new”), krypton (kryptos, “hidden”) and xenon (xenos, “stranger”). Radon was discovered in 1900 by Friedrich Ernst Dorn.

💡 In 1904 Ramsay won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry and Rayleigh the Nobel Prize in Physics, for this work.

✨ Fun facts
• The name “noble gas” (German Edelgas) was coined by chemist Hugo Erdmann in 1898.
• The atomic numbers jump by 8, 8, 18, 18 and 32: 2 → 10 → 18 → 36 → 54 → 86.
• Argon’s symbol used to be just A; it was changed to Ar in 1957.
