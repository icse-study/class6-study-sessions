📖 English Literature · Week 23
Ch 6 · The Arrow and the Song — and the language of Chapter 6
Longfellow’s short poem about where our actions and words end up, plus the chapter’s grammar: types of sentences, question tags, phrasal verbs, collocations, British vs American English and rhyme scheme.
🏹 The poem

Stanza 1: I shot an arrow into the air; it fell to earth, I knew not where — it flew too swiftly for sight to follow.
Stanza 2: I breathed a song into the air; it fell to earth, I knew not where — no one’s sight is keen enough to follow “the flight of song”.
Stanza 3: Long, long afterward, I found the arrow, “still unbroke”, in an oak; and the song, from beginning to end, “in the heart of a friend”.

Meaning: once released, our actions (arrow) and words (song) travel beyond our sight — but they last. Kind words live on in other people’s hearts. Connects with: “Be careful with your words. Once they are said, they can be only forgiven, not forgotten.”

📐 Form

12 lines · 3 stanzas of 4 lines · rhyme scheme aabb: air/where, sight/flight · air/where, strong/song · oak/unbroke, end/friend — six rhyming pairs. Stanzas 1 and 2 are built the same way (parallel structure); stanza 3 answers both.
“Unbroke” is not standard English (unbroken) — poetic licence for the rhyme with “oak”.
Frost’s “Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening” rhymes aaba (know, though, here, snow).

✏️ Grammar of Chapter 6

Sentence types: declarative (statement, .) · interrogative (question, ?) · imperative (instruction, usually .) · exclamatory (strong feeling, !).
Question tags: positive statement → negative tag (“They will come, won’t they?”); negative statement → positive tag (“Amy didn’t want anyone to know, did she?”).
Phrasal verbs: set out (start a journey), look after, let off, pass out (faint), come up with (think of), do away with, get away with.
Verb–noun collocations: make a fire, spread the table, catch a cold, keep a diary, make a mistake, pay a visit, reach a conclusion, take a break.
British vs American: flat/apartment, biscuit/cookie, football/soccer, postbox/mailbox, shop/store, chemist/drugstore · favour/favor, towards/toward, organise/organize, cancelled/canceled, afterwards/afterward.
-ious / -eous: anxious, cautious, obvious, hilarious, ferocious · gaseous, righteous, advantageous, spontaneous, miscellaneous.

✍️ Meet the authors — fun facts & other works

✍️ Henry Wadsworth Longfellow (1807–1882)

American poet, translator and educator, known for musical poems that teach values.

🌟 Fun facts
• He was the first American to translate Dante’s Divine Comedy from Italian into English.
• Famous long poems: The Song of Hiawatha and Evangeline, A Tale of Acadie; also “Paul Revere’s Ride”.
• He taught modern languages at Harvard University.
• He was the first American poet honoured with a bust in Poets’ Corner, Westminster Abbey, London.

✍️ Writing Practice — answer on paper first (not scored)
1. What does the speaker wish to convey through the arrow and the song?
👀 Show model answer
Once we shoot an arrow or sing a song, we cannot see where it goes. In the same way, our actions and words travel far beyond us. The arrow found unbroken in an oak and the song found whole in a friend’s heart show that they last a long time. So we should act and speak with care and kindness.
2. How does the quotation “Be careful with your words…” connect with the poem?
👀 Show model answer
The quotation says words, once spoken, cannot be forgotten — only forgiven. The poem shows the same truth: the song the speaker breathed into the air was remembered, from beginning to end, in a friend’s heart long afterwards. Harsh words would last just as long, so we must choose our words wisely.
