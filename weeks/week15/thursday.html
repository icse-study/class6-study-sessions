Revision 3 — Ratio, Percentage & Mensuration
Weeks 5–8 revision. Ratio and proportion, the unitary method, percentages, and every area and perimeter formula of the year.
➗ The ideas

Ratio and proportion

A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind; it has no unit. A proportion says two ratios are equal.

a : b = c : d → a × d = b × c (product of extremes = product of means)
To divide ₹600 in 2 : 3, total parts = 5, so one part = ₹120.
💡 Always simplify a ratio to its lowest terms: 20 : 30 = 2 : 3.

Unitary method and percentage

Unitary method — find the value of one, then multiply.
Percentage — a fraction with denominator 100.

5 kg cost ₹600 → 1 kg costs ₹120 → 8 kg cost ₹960
15% of 200 = 15/100 × 200 = 30
Percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100
💡 To turn a fraction into a percentage, multiply by 100: 3/4 = 75%.

Mensuration — all the formulas

Rectangle: area = l × b · perimeter = 2(l + b)
Square: area = s × s · perimeter = 4s
Triangle: area = ½ × base × height
Parallelogram: area = base × height
Circle: diameter = 2 × radius
💡 Area is measured in square units (cm²), perimeter in plain units (cm). Mixing them up is the commonest mistake.
🎉 Fun facts & trivia

The per cent sign % grew out of the Italian phrase per cento. Clerks shortened 'cento' to a small circle over a line, and centuries of hurried writing turned it into %.

The metric system was designed so that 1 cm³ of water weighs 1 gram and 1 litre of water weighs 1 kilogram — no conversion tables needed.

Ratios keep maps honest: a scale of 1 : 50,000 means 1 cm on the map is 50,000 cm — half a kilometre — on the ground.

📜 The Year in Numbers · Part 3 of 4 — Measuring With the Body

Before the metre, people measured with their bodies. A foot was a foot, a cubit was elbow to fingertip, and a yard was said to be the distance from King Henry I's nose to his thumb.

The problem is obvious: everybody's arm is different, so a merchant's cubit and a customer's cubit rarely matched. The Egyptians solved this by keeping a royal cubit in granite, against which every wooden measuring rod had to be checked — the world's first standards office.

In 1793 France replaced all of it with the metre, defined from the size of the Earth itself, so that no king's arm could ever change it again.

1️⃣ Pronunciation & liaison (week 2)

Silent endings — remember CaReFuL

Most final consonants are silent: d, g, m, n, p, s, t, x, z.
But C, R, F, L are usually pronounced — think of the English word CaReFuL.

petit (t silent) · parc (c heard) · bonjour (r heard) · neuf (f heard) · avril (l heard)

Letters that change their sound

c before e / i → 's' (ce, merci); before a / o / u → 'k' (comment).
s between two vowels → 'z' (musée).
h is silent: homme sounds like 'om'.

Liaison — linking words

A silent final consonant is sounded when the next word starts with a vowel.

LetterSounds likeExample
s, xzles enfants → lay-z-enfants
dtun grand homme → gran-t-om
fvneuf heures → neu-v-heures
💡 Never link after et (une fille et | un homme) or before an h aspiré (les | héros).
2️⃣ Pronouns, être & avoir (weeks 3–5)

Four traps

• je → j' before a vowel: j'ai, j'aime.
• tu for friends and family; vous for a teacher, an adult you don't know, or more than one person.
• elles = an all-girls group; ils = boys or a mixed group.
• Age uses avoir: J'ai onze ans (literally 'I have eleven years').

💡 il est = he is (être) · il a = he has (avoir). Don't mix them!

4️⃣ Regular -er verbs (week 6)

Stem + ending

Drop -er to get the stem (parler → parl), then add the ending.

PronomEndingparleraimer
je-eje parlej'aime
tu-estu parlestu aimes

ils / elles-entils parlentelles aiment
💡 -e, -es, -ent are silent, so je parle, tu parles and ils parlent all sound the same. Only -ons and -ez are heard.

Watch out

préférer → elles préfèrent (the é becomes è).
aller looks like an -er verb but is irregular: je vais, tu vas, il va, nous allons, vous allez, ils vont.

5️⃣ -ger verbs (week 12)

Only nous changes

Add an e before -ons to keep the soft 'j' sound: nous mangeons, nous voyageons, nous nageons, nous rangeons.

manger · voyager · nager · ranger · corriger · changer · partager · mélanger · bouger · exiger
💡 Vous nagez, ils nagent — no extra e. Only nous gets it.
6️⃣ -ir verbs (week 10)

8️⃣ Adjectives (weeks 7, 11)

Agreement — match the noun

+ e feminine · + s plural · + es feminine plural: grand, grande, grands, grandes.

Already ends in -s / -x? No change for masculine plural (gros, épais, heureux).

Position — BAGS

Most adjectives go after the noun (une maison bleue, un garçon français).
BAGS adjectives go before: Beauty (beau, joli) · Age (jeune, vieux, nouveau) · Goodness (bon, mauvais) · Size (grand, petit, gros).

💡 Before a masculine noun starting with a vowel: un bel homme, un nouvel ami, un vieil arbre.

🔟 Negation & questions (week 14)

ne + verb + pas

Wrap the verb: Je ne parle pas anglais. Before a vowel or silent h: Il n'est pas médecin · Elle n'habite pas à Londres.

💡 Answering a tu question? Use je. A vous question? Use nous: Vous avez le livre ? — Non, nous n'avons pas le livre.

🎯 Fun Trivia
French is an official language in 29 countries — from Canada to Senegal to Switzerland — and is spoken on all five inhabited continents.

The Académie française, founded in 1635, has 40 members nicknamed les Immortels (the Immortals). Their job is to look after the French language and its dictionary.
