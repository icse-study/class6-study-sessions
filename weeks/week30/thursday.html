The Weird and Wonderful World of the Noble Gases
Cool, lesser-known facts that make the topic stick: escaping helium, a xenon surprise, Superman, and the newest noble gas. Then play Noble Gas Jeopardy and take the 25-question quiz.

🚀 Helium the escape artist

Helium is so light that once it escapes into the air it rises to the top of the atmosphere and leaks away into space — Earth’s gravity cannot hold it. New helium is slowly made underground by radioactive decay in rocks, but it is not renewable on human timescales.

💡 Helium balloons go flat faster than air balloons because tiny helium atoms slip through the rubber.

⚗️ The noble exception — 1962

Everyone thought noble gases could never react. In 1962, Neil Bartlett made the first noble gas compound, using xenon and a very strong fluorine compound. So noble gases are not totally “lazy” — just very picky. The big, heavy ones (krypton, xenon, radon) can react; helium and neon never have.

💡 Heavier atoms hold their outer electrons more loosely, so they are a little easier to persuade.

🛰️ Xenon’s wild jobs

Xenon is used as an anaesthetic (to put patients to sleep for surgery) and as the propellant in ion engines that push spacecraft through space.

🦸 Krypton and Superman

Superman (created in 1938) comes from the planet Krypton and is weakened by Kryptonite. The real element was discovered 40 years earlier, in 1898 — the comic just borrowed the cool name. From 1960 to 1983, the metre was officially defined using the orange-red light given out by krypton-86.

🆕 The newest noble gas

Oganesson (Og), element 118, sits at the bottom of Group 18. It is made in laboratories, only a few atoms have ever existed, and it is radioactive. It is named after the Russian scientist Yuri Oganessian — one of only two elements named after a living person.

🧊 Super-strange helium

Helium is the second most common element in the universe, after hydrogen. It is the only element that will not turn solid at normal pressure, even close to absolute zero. Very cold liquid helium becomes a superfluid that can creep up the walls of its container!

🔢 Electron arrangements

Neon: 2, 8 · Argon: 2, 8, 8 — full outer shells. Noble gases have a valency of 0, which is why Group 18 used to be called Group 0.

• Neon is about the fifth most common element in the universe, but rare on Earth.
