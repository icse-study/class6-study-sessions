➗ The ideas

How to attack a circle problem

1. Draw it. Even a rough circle with the centre marked sorts out half the confusion.
2. Turn diameters into radii (or the other way round) before you start.
3. Name every line you are given — radius, chord, diameter, secant or tangent.
4. Compare distances with r whenever a point's position is asked.

💡 In construction questions, the phrase 'circle with A as centre passing through B' means AB is a radius.

Constructions worth practising

With a compass and ruler:
• Draw a circle of radius 4.2 cm; mark A on it, join AO and extend to meet the circle at B → AB is a diameter, 8.4 cm.
• Draw a circle of radius 6 cm and a chord AB = 6 cm; join OA and OB → triangle AOB is equilateral, so ∠AOB = 60°.
• Draw a line AB = 8.4 cm as a diameter, mark C on the circle and join AC, BC → ∠ACB = 90°.

A chord equal to the radius always gives a 60° angle at the centre.

The traps

• A chord of 11 cm is impossible in a circle of radius 5 cm — the diameter is only 10 cm.
• 'Twice the radius' and 'half the diameter' describe the same length.
• The major segment is the one that contains the centre.

🎉 Fun facts & trivia

An angle drawn in a semi-circle is always a right angle — a fact known as Thales' theorem, after the Greek thinker Thales of Miletus, around 600 BCE. Mark any point on a semi-circle, join it to both ends of the diameter, and you get exactly 90°.

Bicycle wheels were once made in wildly different sizes — the penny-farthing had a front wheel of nearly 1.5 m across, because a bigger wheel travelled further per pedal turn before gears were invented.

The Romans laid out their army camps using a circle-based instrument called a groma; straight roads came from carefully placed circles.

📜 Part 4 of 12 — Why Wheels Beat Rollers

Before wheels, heavy stones were moved on rollers — loose logs placed under a sledge. It worked, but someone had to keep running to the back to fetch the log that popped out and carry it to the front.

The wheel-and-axle fixed that by keeping the round thing attached to the cart. The catch was friction at the axle, which is why early carts were slow. The real leap came thousands of years later with the ball bearing — tiny spheres that let the axle roll instead of rub.

Every one of those improvements depends on the same property: constant distance from a centre.
