Circles — Arc, Sector & Quadrant
Chapter 17 · Sections 17.6–17.7. Pieces of the boundary, and the slices of pizza they cut out.
➗ The ideas

Arc

An arc is a part of the circumference — a piece of the curve itself, not of the space inside.

Join any two points A and B on a circle and the circumference splits into two arcs. The shorter one is the minor arc, the longer one the major arc.

Arc APB (shorter) = minor arc · Arc AQB (longer) = major arc
💡 An arc is a length along the curve. A sector and a segment are regions with area.

Sector

A sector is the part of a circle enclosed by two radii and an arc — a pizza slice.

Two radii cut the circle into a minor sector (smaller) and a major sector (larger).

Radii OA, OB + arc APB → sector OAPB

Quadrant

A quadrant is the special sector made by two perpendicular radii — the angle at the centre is 90°.

Four quadrants make one whole circle.

Quarter of a circle → angle at the centre = 90°
💡 'Quad' means four, as in quadrilateral and quadruple.

Sector or segment?

Both are regions inside the circle, but the boundary tells them apart:
• Sector → bounded by two radii and an arc.
• Segment → bounded by a chord and an arc.

Two straight edges from the centre → sector. One straight edge not through the centre → segment.
🎉 Fun facts & trivia

A pizza cut into 8 equal slices gives 8 sectors, each with a 45° angle at the centre — because 360 ÷ 8 = 45.

A protractor is a semi-circle marked from 0° to 180°; a full circle protractor goes to 360°. The number 360 comes from ancient Babylon, whose astronomers counted in sixties and reckoned the year at about 360 days.

The Sydney Opera House roof is built from sections of a single imaginary sphere of radius 75 m — the architect's trick that made the curved shells possible to build.

📜 Part 5 of 12 — Dividing the Circle Into 360

Why 360 degrees, and not 100? The answer goes back to Babylon, about 4,000 years ago. Babylonian mathematicians counted in base 60, and 360 = 6 × 60 divides neatly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12 and more.

That makes it wonderfully convenient: half a circle is 180°, a quarter is 90°, a third is 120°, a sixth is 60° — all whole numbers. A 100-degree circle would give ugly fractions for the commonest slices.

So every time you measure a quadrant as 90°, you are using a decision made by astronomers who watched the sky from mud-brick towers.
