Integers — Addition & Subtraction on the Number Line
Chapter 4 · Section 4.5. Every sum here is a walk along the number line: which way do you step, and how far?
➗ Adding on the number line

Which way do I move?

Start at the first number, then:

Adding a positive number → move right
Adding a negative number → move left

Example: (+3) + (−4) → start at +3, move 4 steps left → −1

The shortcut rules

Same signs: add the numbers and keep the common sign.

(−5) + (−8) = −13  ·  (+5) + (+8) = +13

Different signs: subtract the smaller from the larger and keep the sign of the larger number.

(+7) + (−10) = −3  (10 − 7 = 3, and 10 is negative)
(−4) + (+6) = +2  (6 − 4 = 2, and 6 is positive)
➗ Subtracting on the number line

Add the opposite

Subtracting a positive → move left.
Subtracting a negative → move right, because taking away a negative is the same as adding a positive.

(+8) − (−5) = 8 + 5 = +13
(−8) − (−5) = −8 + 5 = −3
(−7) − (+12) = −7 − 12 = −19
💡 The single rule that covers every subtraction: change the sign of the number being subtracted, then add.

Reading the words correctly

"5 more than −3" → −3 + 5 = 2
"7 less than −2" → −2 − 7 = −9
"Subtract −4 from −9" → −9 − (−4) = −9 + 4 = −5

💡 "Subtract a from b" always means b − a, not a − b. Read the sentence twice before you write the sum.
Before the quiz
Rewrite every subtraction as an addition first — (−8) − (−5) becomes (−8) + 5. Once there are no minus-signs-in-front-of-brackets left, the sign rules do the rest.

📜 The Story of Algebra · Part 2 of 16 — India Invents the Numbers You Are Adding Today

🇮🇳 Brahmagupta, 628 CE

The Indian mathematician Brahmagupta was the first to write down systematic rules for zero, for negative numbers, and for equations — the exact things you are adding and subtracting today.

He wrote rules such as: a debt subtracted from zero is a fortune; a fortune subtracted from zero is a debt.
💡 Read that again with modern symbols: 0 − (−5) = +5 and 0 − (+5) = −5. You are using his rule every time you flip a sign.

Fortune and debt — the first number line

Brahmagupta thought of positives as fortune (money you have) and negatives as debt (money you owe). Adding a debt to a fortune cancels part of it; adding debt to debt makes a bigger debt.

Fortune ₹10 + debt ₹4 → 10 + (−4) = 6  ·  debt ₹5 + debt ₹8 → (−5) + (−8) = −13

Zero was the controversial part

Treating zero as a number — not just an empty space — was revolutionary. Many traditions refused to accept it. Without zero there is no number line, no place value, and no modern computing.

💡 Negative numbers were rejected for centuries too — people said negative apples made no sense. They only won once debts, temperatures and directions needed them.

Force — push, pull and what it can do
Concise Physics pages 36–42. What a force is, the six things a force can do, how forces add up, units, and the two families of forces: contact and non-contact.
⚡ 1. Force as push, pull, stretch and squeeze
KindWhat it doesExamples from the textbook
Pushmoves a bodypushing a door open, a broom pushing rubbish, a bulldozer pushing rubble, pushing a stroller or a car that won't start

So what is a force?

A force is that cause which changes the state of a body (the state of rest, the state of motion, or the direction of motion) or changes the size or shape of the body.

💡 Short version: force = a push or a pull.

Rest and motion

Rest: the body does not change its position with time, compared with its surroundings.
Motion: the body changes its position with time, compared with its surroundings.

Balanced force — does not change rest to motion or motion to rest.
Unbalanced force — does change the state of rest or motion.
⚡ 2. The six effects of a force
A force can…HowExample
1. move a body at restpush or pull ita kicked ball starts rolling; a pushed car starts moving
2. stop a moving bodyact opposite to the motionbrakes stop a bicycle; a fielder catches a cricket ball
3. make it move fasteract in the direction of motionpedalling harder; pushing a moving swing
4. slow it downact opposite to the motionapplying brakes; pulling a swing back
5. change its directionact at an angle to the motionkicking a moving football; a hockey stick deflecting the ball; hitting a shuttlecock back
6. change its shape or sizeact on a body that can't movesqueezing rubber; stretching a spring

🧪 Activity — change of direction

Hold a small piece of cardboard between two fingers and let it fall. While it is falling, flick it with another finger. It lands somewhere else — the push changed its direction.

🧪 Spring balance

Hang a body from a spring and the spring gets longer. The increase in length is directly proportional to the weight of the body. A spring balance uses this idea to measure weight.

💡 Double the weight → double the stretch.
⚡ 3. Six facts about force

Learn these word for word

1. A force does not change the mass of a body.
2. We cannot see a force — we only see or feel its effects.
3. A force needs both magnitude (how big) and direction.
4. Two forces in the same direction are added. In opposite directions they are subtracted, and the result acts in the direction of the bigger force.
5. Two equal and opposite forces give a resultant of zero — these are balanced forces.
6. S.I. unit: newton (N). Gravitational units: kgf and gf. 1 kgf = 1000 gf, and 1 kgf ≈ 10 N.

Mass or weight?

Mass is measured in kg or g.
Weight is a force — the pull of the earth on a body — so it is measured in kgf or gf (or newtons).

💡 Saying 'my weight is 35 kg' is common, but the correct physics is 35 kgf.

⚡ 5. Non-contact forces (forces from a distance)
Non-contact forceAttract or repel?Example
Gravitationalalways attractsleaves and fruits fall; a ball thrown up comes down; rivers flow downstream

Distance matters

Non-contact forces get weaker as the bodies move apart. Double the distance → the force becomes one-fourth.

Distance × 2 → force ÷ 4 · Distance × 3 → force ÷ 9

🎯 Fun Trivia
• The newton is named after Sir Isaac Newton. One newton is roughly the weight of a small apple — fitting, for the man famous for an apple falling on his head!
• A rocket like Chandrayaan must beat two forces to reach space: the earth's gravity and the friction of the air.
• Gravity is the weakest of the forces in nature, yet it holds the Moon in orbit and keeps your feet on the ground.
